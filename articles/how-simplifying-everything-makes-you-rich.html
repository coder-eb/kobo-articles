Complexity Is for Idiots

Most people, when asked directly, will say they believe complexity is overrated. Yet in practice, individuals and organizations reliably drift toward more elaborate systems, bigger teams, and longer feature lists. Varun Mayya, entrepreneur and co-founder of EOS — a company now approaching 600 employees — argues that this drift is not just inefficient but actively dangerous, and that learning to fight it is one of the most valuable skills a person can develop.

The irony, he notes, is visible in the habits of the people who have figured this out. As founders and executives become wealthier and more experienced, they tend to strip their lives down rather than expand them. Mark Zuckerberg’s rotation of identical gray T-shirts is the most commonly cited example, but the pattern is nearly universal among financially successful entrepreneurs. The motivation is not aesthetic minimalism or personal branding — it is functional. Running a complex business consumes enormous cognitive bandwidth, and trying to simultaneously run a complex personal life is a recipe for failure.

“It’s pointless living a sophisticated, complicated life. It adds more cognitive effort on you. However, the simplest kind of company you can possibly run will most likely end up working best for you.”

The paradox Mayya identifies is that sophistication — the appearance of complexity — is something many people actively seek, particularly early in their careers. It signals expertise. It justifies a profession. But seasoned entrepreneurs almost universally arrive at the opposite conclusion: simplicity is the destination, not a shortcut.

Steve Jobs and the Product Purge

The most instructive historical example of deliberate simplification at scale is Apple under Steve Jobs. When Jobs returned to the company in 1997, Apple had over 70 active products. He cut that lineup to four focused offerings. The decision was not arbitrary — it reflected a precise understanding of how human attention actually works.

Mayya uses what he calls the “toilet model” to describe real-world content consumption. The image most communicators carry in their heads is of an engaged audience sitting attentively, taking notes. The more accurate model is a person multitasking on the toilet — phone in one hand, doing three things at once, with a parent calling in the background. Under those conditions, the fraction of any message that actually lands is somewhere around 0.1 to 0.2 percent.

Jobs understood the same principle applied to product lines. Seventy products require telling seventy consistent stories. The human brain, at least with respect to a single brand, cannot hold seventy stories. Four is manageable. The reduction also had an internal benefit: with seventy initiatives, no one inside the company fully knew what team seventy was doing. Four focused offerings aligned everyone.

Mayya sees the same dynamic playing out in his own business. As EOS has grown, he has deliberately shut down projects that impose unnecessary cognitive tax, choosing instead to concentrate on a small number of flagship priorities.

How Complexity Enters Organizations

Complexity does not arrive by invitation — it seeps in through people. This is the counterintuitive part of scaling a team: every new hire is intended to reduce the founder’s burden, but often increases total organizational friction.

Mayya observed this directly in one of his teams. Each time a new person joined, the work that had previously belonged to one person was split among two. Add a third, and it divided again. The timelines never shortened. Headcount grew, but deadlines stayed exactly the same, because every additional person introduced new communication overhead. Team members started waiting on each other rather than executing. “I’m waiting on this other person” became the defining phrase of large-team inefficiency.

Jeff Bezos’s framework addresses precisely this problem. His “two-pizza rule” holds that any team should be small enough to be fed by two large pizzas — roughly six to ten people. Beyond that threshold, communication costs compound faster than output grows. The rule applies not just to team size but to team mission: each two-pizza unit should own exactly one goal. Multiple goals fragment focus, and fragmented focus guarantees mediocrity across all of them.

At 600 employees, EOS is not immune to this dynamic. Mayya estimates that approximately 50 people are truly critical to the company — the individuals whose departure would allow accumulated complexity to overwhelm their respective teams. The implicit goal he sets for everyone else in the organization is to move toward that group: to become someone who reduces complexity rather than installs it.

One Person, One Task

A related principle operates at the individual level. Mayya attributes this formulation to an entrepreneur he respects: one task, one person.

The temptation, especially when cash flows are tight, is to fill an employee’s slack time with additional assignments. If someone is working six hours and has two free, give them another project. The result, counterintuitively, is substandard work — even from talented people. The moment a capable person has two responsibilities, accountability diffuses. They have a ready excuse for any shortfall, and their cognitive resources split in ways that degrade both outputs.

“The minute you give one good talent two things to do, they are screwed up.”

Give the same person a single task, and something different happens. Their accountability sharpens because there is nowhere else to point. Their quality of work rises, often dramatically. The bottleneck in their thinking clears. This approach makes a company more expensive per unit of output in the short term, because adding responsibilities requires adding people rather than loading existing ones. But it lowers organizational complexity even as headcount grows, because the map of who owns what remains legible.

The implication for managers who are juggling assignments is uncomfortable: the correct response may not be to hire more people, but to do fewer things and do them better.

The Compounding Cost of Features

Software development is where complexity worship is most concentrated and most damaging. Mayya describes a near-universal pattern among engineers in their twenties: a drive to build elaborate codebases, accumulate features, and write more lines of code — in part because volume signals effort and skill. By the time those same engineers reach their thirties, many arrive at the opposite instinct: strip it back, make it legible, keep it maintainable.

The cost of ignoring that instinct is visible in the lifecycle of Scenes, a community platform Mayya founded and later sold to Unacademy. The product suffered from weak product-market fit — users would try it but would migrate to simpler alternatives like WhatsApp groups whenever a competing option appeared. The team’s response was to add features. Users would request a new capability, imply that they would commit to the platform once it was built, and then return to WhatsApp anyway after it shipped. The testing suite grew so tangled that only one developer understood a specific part of it. Hiring, onboarding, and roadmapping all became harder.

“Complexity can be a crutch for lack of PMF or distribution.”

The pattern repeats across app developers everywhere. A developer builds one genuinely useful feature, watches it attract users, and then — unsure what to do next — adds nine more features nobody asked for. The correct move, Mayya argues, is to stop building and start selling. If a feature works, the job is to market and distribute it, not to disturb it with additions. Successful entrepreneurs, in his observation, recognize the one thing that works and pour all remaining energy into marketing and sales. They stop building unless something breaks.

Simplicity as Competitive Advantage

The clearest real-world illustration of simplicity winning in a competitive market is the iPhone versus Android comparison. Android offers more features, more customization, more configurability — and by technical measures, more power. Yet in the United States, the primary market that most businesses target, the iPhone continues to dominate. Mayya, a longtime Android advocate, concluded in retrospect that he never actually used 99 percent of Android’s additional features. The audience that iPhone captures has reached the same conclusion.

The same dynamic explains why Mayya’s “God in a Box” app — a wrapper connecting ChatGPT’s API to WhatsApp, launched the day the API became available — attracted millions of users despite being, in his own description, the dumbest and simplest thing he had ever built.

This connects to a broader argument about the value of wrappers and abstractions. Building on top of existing technology rather than from first principles is not laziness — it is how complexity gets managed at civilizational scale. Mayya uses the example of making indigo paint from scratch: the process is so involved that the entire exercise would be consumed by it. Painters today work on top of centuries of pigment refinement, canvas manufacturing, and tool production. Startups exist for the same reason: they build on top of infrastructure that larger, more complex organizations no longer have the bandwidth to extend, because those organizations are spending all their cognitive resources holding their existing systems together.

Knowing When Not to Add

The iPad editing episode inside EOS illustrates how well-intentioned ideas can carry hidden complexity costs that the proposer never factors in. One team member repeatedly argued that the company should switch from desktop editing to iPads. The idea had personal merit — he had tried it and found it effective. But running the proposal through full organizational logic revealed a cascade of consequences: the hiring pipeline would break, because candidates trained in Adobe Premiere would find their skills suddenly irrelevant; the software environment would fragment, with some editors using one suite and others using another; peripheral equipment — styluses — would introduce new categories of loss and replacement that don’t apply to mice. The switch would have been viable only if made at founding, when no dependencies existed yet.

The question Mayya wants his managers to ask of every proposed change is not “is this a good idea in isolation?” but “does this make the process simpler or more complex?” Ideas that simplify get implemented. Ideas that add complexity — regardless of their theoretical sophistication — get declined.

He extends the same logic to automation and AI. The temptation to automate before fully understanding a task manually is a form of premature complexity. His standing rule: before deploying AI avatars for content creation, shoot real content for six months first. Before building a tool to automate video editing, edit ten videos yourself to understand the full range of the problem. Paul Graham’s principle — do things that don’t scale — applies here. Manual execution first, automation second, only once the manual version is thoroughly understood.

The Managerial Discipline of Subtraction

Mayya’s final prescription is aimed at managers who have recently gained the authority to implement systems and find themselves reaching for complicated solutions because complexity signals seriousness.

The three-part test he applies to everything inside EOS is whether a given system is easy to understand, easy to maintain, and easy to fix. A recently installed LAN cabling system with a NAS loop that only the IT manager comprehends fails all three. When pressed on why it was implemented, the answer was essentially that professional companies use this kind of infrastructure — a social justification, not a functional one.

That social pressure — the desire to appear sophisticated, to use the tools that serious organizations use — is the underlying mechanism by which most complexity enters companies. Combating it requires managers to ask first-principles questions rather than defaulting to industry convention.

“You should not be the source of complexity. You should not be the person coming and implementing ideas that just require too much time, energy, bandwidth to create where nobody has those things.”

Mayya’s observation, borne out repeatedly across his teams, is that almost every manager who resists this advice eventually comes around — typically within two years, after living through the consequences. His goal in laying out these principles is to compress that timeline, so the problems of unnecessary complexity can be avoided rather than recovered from.
