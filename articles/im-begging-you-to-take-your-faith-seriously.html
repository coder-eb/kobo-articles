The Spiritual Allergy to Practical Advice

Allergies are annoying. Some can even be deadly, but I think that there is a sort of spiritual allergy we can have in the church. So, I got thinking about this because last year I heard a pastor say something like evangelicals are allergic to the practical. And I thought, that’s a great way to put it.

As you can imagine, speaking about personal productivity to Christians is a very practical ministry, and I’ve been doing this now for 10 years, writing books on it, talking videos, doing podcasts, writing articles. I get a front row seat to the allergic reaction of evangelicals to practical advice. Because frequently Christians, even those who love sound teaching from the Bible and good theology, as soon as you get practical, as soon as you get into application, they get a little crazy.

And I’ve thought a lot about this. Why is it that we can love theology but not love application? Because I think that’s what’s happening. We like principles. We like truth, which we should. We like doctrine. This is good stuff. We like orthodoxy, but when it comes to orthopraxy, that’s where we’re like, “Hey, hey, hey, calm down. Don’t be legalistic. Don’t you tell me what to do.”

Why the Church Avoids Practical Teaching

So, I was actually a guest on someone’s podcast the other day, and we were talking about this very thing because she talks about practical stuff, I talk about practical stuff. And she’s like, “Why is it that the church doesn’t talk about this stuff?” And so, my take on it is that one, it’s not really practical possible to do this from the pulpit. When you are preaching a sermon in a church, you’re trying to do things that are going to be broadly applicable to everybody in the pew. If you’re speaking to a couple hundred people or a couple thousand people in a church sermon, you can’t get down to the nitty-gritty. Beyond that like basic prescription, you can’t get more detailed because you’re not one-on-one with a person. You don’t know their life. You don’t have the details of it. So, I think that’s a big hole in it.

And this is normally stuff you would have gotten from your parents or from someone who is discipling or mentoring you and in many churches that just isn’t happening. And so there’s you don’t have people speaking into your individual life that would show you the practical stuff of life. This is one of the reasons this channel exists. I’m trying to help Christians learn some of that stuff they wouldn’t have gotten because I didn’t get a lot of this stuff. I grew up, my dad died when I was just a kid and so I didn’t have some of that, you know, how do you do this with your finances? How do you manage your time? How do you deal with these little situations? That’s stuff that should happen more in a discipleship one-on-one relationship. It’s more natural there than in like a sermon from a pulpit. I get that. So I’m not blaming churches or blaming pastors for not being more practical with this.

But here is where I do think it’s strange. The same fog of applicational vagueness hangs over Christian content more generally. There’s a genre of Christian books called Christian living. I often think it should be called the theory of Christian living. People are scared to death to say, here is a framework, here is a formula, you should follow this.

The Fear of Legalism and Its Overcorrection

Why? Where does the allergy come from? I think the generous reading is it comes from a fear of legalism, which is a good fear to have. We don’t want to be legalistic like the Pharisees who were making up extra rules and saying, here’s how a Christian must live to be right with God. Don’t do that. And that’s hopefully you don’t hear me saying that when I tell you here’s how to keep a schedule and stuff. But I think that fear of legalism leads us to the allergy to the practical. We don’t think it all the way through. We overcorrect away from that which would really serve people. And if someone does dare to get specific about here’s exactly what you should do, they bury it in like a mountain of caveats so much that it dulls and blunts the edge of it so that no one’s going to do anything with it.

Let me give you an example. So recently I was at church talking to a brother in Christ in the hallways and he said something to me about, hey, do you want to know my like unpopular take? He said, I think nearly every Christian should delete their social media. Even as I say that, I can hear you tapping away on your keyboards or on your thumbs on your phone about to say, hey, that’s legalistic. You can’t tell me what to do. What about this? What about this? What about this? And the reason I know you’re thinking that is because that’s exactly what I thought in the moment. And now if you know me, you follow my stuff, I agree with that statement. Like I haven’t had social media besides YouTube for this channel in years. So like I agree with it, but my point is that allergic reaction.

Why, when he said something specific, did I say in my heart, don’t don’t don’t don’t say that or here’s all the exceptions. Why did I run to the exceptions first? Why didn’t I take the nugget of wisdom that was there and be like, yeah, that’s true. Because even in the way he said it, he said, I think. So he’s saying it’s an opinion. And he said nearly all Christians, not every Christian. And so he even caveated it in the way he said it. It wasn’t that strong of a take, to be honest. And I agreed with it. And even still, my mind was like, well, let’s think, what about the missionaries? Well, what about people who need this? What about — okay, yeah, true. That was all covered in the statement. Why though?

My question is, why do we feel the need to qualify everything into oblivion? Why can’t we just speak straight and say, this is wisdom, this is not. Generally speaking, living this way is better than living this way.

Another one, debt. So Dave Ramsey, right? Financial Peace University, all that stuff online. I like Dave Ramsey. But he teaches very simple principles, the baby steps, right? And he helps people get out of debt and save up and build wealth and it’s great stuff, right? But all the time you go to the comments on any video and they’re like, well, actually, step number seven, that wouldn’t apply if you were from Libya and you were an expat living — and you’re just like, what? What is — why are we — what? Even just the principle, the general principle he often cites from the scripture, he’s like, “The debtor is the slave to the lender.” And he says, “Debt is bad.” And people say, “Well, what about good debt? What about good debt?” And it’s like, it’s a general principle. Why can’t we just accept that as wisdom and move on? Why do we have this allergic reaction to prescription?

Confusing Sin Issues with Wisdom Principles

I think one of the big things behind this is we tend to conflate sin issues and wisdom issues. For Christians especially, anytime someone says, “Hey, you should do this and not this.” We say, “Are you saying I’m sinning?” Which one, calm down.

So, scripture does forbid some things as violations of God’s law. They’re sins against God, sins against your fellow man. They’re wrong objectively, always. But we also have a category in the Bible of wisdom. You think of Proverbs, you think of Ecclesiastes, you think of even James, like wisdom literature we call it. These are often full of principles that are generally true, that are going to lead to good outcomes. The debt thing is an example of a wisdom principle. Don’t get into debt. It’s not a universal thing. He’s not saying it’s sin if you do. It just says, “Hey, you’re bringing problems on yourself you don’t need.” So, in as far as you can avoid it, you probably should. That’s wisdom. These wisdom principles don’t carry the same weight of absolutes as a sin issue does.

But I think even beyond the legalism and the sin wisdom thing, there’s just the fact that we’re prideful. When someone says, “Hey, I think this is a wiser way to live.” We take it personally. If we’re not living that way, we take it personally. You see it in the comments of my videos, you see it in the comments of the Dave Ramsey videos, too. Where they’re just like, “Well, I’m not doing that. What are you — you saying I’m dumb? You saying —” It’s like, dude, this is a video. I don’t even know you. Like, get out of — like, I’m not accusing you of anything. Why are you taking this personally? Just calm down. They’re just saying that generally this is a good way to do things. So, if you want to take that advice, go for it. If it doesn’t work in your situation, that’s fine. Relax. Even advice that’s explicitly framed as an opinion, we react poorly to. We have that allergic reaction of “How dare you?”

But my whole point in this rant or whatever this is, is whether it’s a fear of legalism thing or fear of man or all of us just being sensitive little snowflakes, the net result of our allergy to the practical is that our faith gets reduced to theory.

Faith Without Practice Is Arrested Development

I think this is one of the biggest issues right now in evangelicalism. We love theology. We love doctrine. We’re not living it. I don’t mean that you were all out here living in like wanton crazy abject sin. I just mean that it’s all theory. It’s all cerebral. It’s not put into practice. And so here we find ourselves living in this age of prolific Christian content. I can get wonderful sermons from around the world. I can watch wonderful YouTube channels and podcasts and read great articles from great thinkers who love God and love the word and I can learn and learn and learn. And yet even the most obvious practical advice has to be soaked in this brine of qualifications and caveats before being presented as here’s a mere suggestion. And so you’ve got these sort of evangelical — I call them the merchants of milk toast — that churn out this content of books and podcasts and articles all saying the same stuff on repeat but never anything concrete. Good doctrine, but it’s just up here. It’s never down here at the level of now what do I do with this? It’s an anemic faith that spills a lot of ink but never a drop of sweat.

The problem I have with theoretical Christian living is that it reduces our faith from a walk down to a notion. It’s an idea. It’s a philosophy. Christianity is not a philosophy. It is doctrine, yes, but it is a way of life. That is why frequently in the New Testament it’s compared to a walk. It’s how you live. It’s how you conduct yourself. It is doctrine made into devotion. It’s real-life stuff. What I don’t want is any notion of the Christian life that never has to leave the armchair. Where it’s like, “Well, let’s just debate eschatology. Let’s debate apologetics. Let’s debate this.” Again, all important stuff. I’m not denigrating that. I’m just saying it’s more than that. You have to actually live it out.

So, while we all agree, yes, we should think differently from the world, God help the man who suggests that we should live differently from the world. You know, the place where I see this the most in my content is the stuff that veers towards digital minimalism. As I’ve taken steps in my own life, say, “Hey, this has been helpful to me to reduce my dependence on phones or the internet or modern conveniences in small ways.” Guys, I’m not Amish here. I’m recording this in an absurdly complex environment of cameras. But, my point is if I suggest, “Hey, this stuff is stealing our attention. This is a problem. This might be a worship issue.” People come out of the woodwork to say, “Well, no, that wouldn’t be practical. That wouldn’t really work in my situation. I can’t do that or I can’t do this.”

And I get it. Don’t get me wrong. I’m ranting a little bit here. But, don’t get me wrong. Your situation might not allow for it. My point is that the Christian life is a radical thing. If we can’t move beyond basic discomfort in like the smallest way, be like, “Well, that would be inconvenient. Certainly the Lord wouldn’t want me to be inconvenienced.” Can you imagine the New Testament apostles talking that way? Or the early Christian martyrs? Like, “Lord, I get that you don’t want me to deny the faith, but do you realize that they’re going to burn me at the stake and that’d be inconvenient.” Like, what is it that you are willing to give up? What is the change that we’re willing to do that would be a little bit different from the world? What if faithfulness required you to not be on social media if it’s a problem for you? Would you do it? Or would you say, “Well, but then what about Facebook Marketplace?” It’s like, why is our bar so low? Why is it we won’t change the smallest thing? Why is it the Christian life can live so comfortably at the level of theory? There’s something wrong.

It’s an arrested development if your Christian life is only theory, only theology, only doctrine, only Bible study. It would be like somebody joins the military and they go to boot camp and they’re always training, training, training, training, training, but they’re like, “Well, I’m not actually going to go to the war. I’m not going to go to battle. I’m not going to the front lines. I’m here to do the training.” It’s like, no, do you miss it — the training is for the battle. And the training of the Christian life is being with the saints on Sunday, worshipping, hearing the word preached, being in prayer with other believers, being in your own private prayer, studying the word by yourself. That stuff’s important in and of itself. It is. You’re in fellowship with God, you’re learning, but the point is it’s an equipping. It’s training. It’s the boot camp. The living is the rest of life. And if it has no impact on the way you live besides, “Well, I don’t cuss like my coworkers,” then something’s gone wrong.

The Christian faith is not a stationary thing. It’s ambulatory. It is a walk. Yes, it starts in the pew, but it must move out to the pavement.

A Call for Specific, Practical Faithfulness

It is not legalism to pursue holiness. It’s not legalism to set a goal to grow in some area of faithfulness. It’s not legalism to have your alarm set a little earlier so you can get a jump on your morning routines and being in the word. It’s not legalism to say, “I’m not going to use this certain type of technology because I think it’s detrimental to my Christian faith.” It’s not legalism even to suggest that it could be wise for others to do the same. Obviously, we don’t bind consciences. Obviously, we don’t say, “Hey, everyone has to do this or you’re in sin.” We do this stuff to like earn extra God’s love or be right with him or something like that. We do it because we’re trying to be faithful. We’re trying to live out our faith. We do it because we are already loved, chosen in him from before the foundations of the world, redeemed by the blood of the lamb. We’re new creations in Christ Jesus, indwelt by the Spirit, called to live out good works, to bear fruit, and bear them abundantly for his glory and for the love of our neighbor. And that means living differently.

That’s not legalism. The standard isn’t to live exactly how the world lives and not be too weird. The standard is I want to be holy as he is holy. I want to give myself fully to him. I want to love him with all my heart, with all my soul, with all my mind, with all my strength. That’s going to change what I do. I’m going to look different from the world. I’m going to be a weirdo. Good. Let’s be weirdos together because Christianity is a living faith. It’s an active faith.

So, can we please be specific? Please tell me what is working for you practically. What helps you to be consistent in the word? What helps you to be better at sharing your faith? What helps you to be better at storing away God’s word in your mind? What helps you to live in a way that’s pleasing to God Monday through Friday, Saturday, and Sunday? How do you manage your time to his glory? How do you set goals and actually reach them? How do you — actually nitty-gritty, what do you do to mortify sin in your life? Don’t just give me principles. Please give me practicals. I want them. I need them. I’m begging for them.

And that’s what I want to do for you guys too with this channel, with my content, with Redeeming Productivity, with the academy. I’m not trying to be legalistic. I’m trying to be specific. I’m trying to be practical. Because for whatever reason, somehow this is something that is just lacking in the evangelical world right now. And I’m putting this out there because I don’t think I’m alone in wanting a practical faith that actually changes how I live. Even when it’s uncomfortable, even when it puts me out of step with the world. And even if sometimes it makes my brothers and sisters in Christ sneeze.
